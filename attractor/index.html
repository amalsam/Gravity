Strange Attractors simulation

Interactive Lorenz, Rössler and Thomas strange attractor simulation. Watch three paths that start 0.001 apart diverge across the attractor — the butterfly effect — traced with RK4 integration in rotating 3D.

What you’re looking at

Strange attractors are the shapes that chaotic systems settle onto. This simulation integrates three classic systems of differential equations — Lorenz, Rössler and Thomas — with fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) and draws the trajectory as a glowing 3D trail. The path never repeats and never crosses itself, yet it stays confined to a recognisable shape.

How to use it

  1. 1Pick an attractor: Lorenz, Rössler or Thomas. The faint outline shows the whole shape.
  2. 2Watch the three coloured paths: they start 0.001 apart and soon end up on completely different parts of the attractor.
  3. 3Adjust trail length and speed, turn auto-rotate on or off, and restart the paths to see it again.

Questions

What is the Lorenz attractor?

+

A set of three differential equations Edward Lorenz derived from a simplified model of atmospheric convection in 1963: dx/dt = σ(y − x), dy/dt = x(ρ − z) − y, dz/dt = xy − βz. With σ = 10, ρ = 28 and β = 8/3 its solutions trace the famous butterfly-shaped attractor.

What makes an attractor 'strange'?

+

Its trajectories are chaotic — nearby starting points diverge exponentially — yet they stay on a bounded shape with a fractal structure. That sensitive dependence on initial conditions is the butterfly effect.

Why use RK4 integration?

+

Fourth-order Runge–Kutta samples the slope four times per step, which is far more accurate than simple Euler steps for the same step size and keeps the chaotic trajectory on the attractor.
